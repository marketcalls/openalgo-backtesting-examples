Which NIFTY 50 stocks move least alike?

A diversified portfolio needs holdings that do not rise and fall on the same days. This page measures how alike every pair of NIFTY 50 stocks moved over five years, picks the that move least alike, and explains each step so you can repeat it.

How to select low-correlation stocks, step by step

The method has eight steps. Each one says what was done and why, so you can apply it to any group of stocks.

  1. Use clean daily closes

    Correlation compares how prices change from one day to the next, so every stock needs complete, comparable data. Daily closes from Historify cover all stocks over the same trading days (JIOFIN only from its listing in August 2023, days).

    Why daily, not 1-minute. On 1-minute candles, stocks that react to the same news a few seconds apart look less related than they are, and the noise of single trades drowns the pattern. Daily closes measure what a positional investor actually holds through.

    Why check for jumps. A split, bonus or demerger shows as a price drop that nobody lost money on, and it would distort every pair that stock is in. These prices are already adjusted for splits and bonuses; one day is left out: . Real crashes, such as the Adani falls of early 2023 and the election-result day of 4 Jun 2024, stay in. Those are exactly the days diversification is for.

  2. Compare daily returns, not prices

    Turn each price series into daily percentage changes, then compare those.

    Why. Two stocks that both doubled over five years have price charts that look alike even if their good and bad days never matched. Daily returns ask the useful question: on the days one stock rose, did the other rise too?

  3. Measure every pair

    The correlation of two return series runs from +1 (always move together) through 0 (unrelated) to -1 (always opposite). With stocks there are pairs, and the average pair is .

    What it shows. NIFTY 50 stocks mostly move a little alike, because the whole market reacts to the same news. In the map, grouped by sector, the red blocks along the diagonal are businesses that move together: the most alike pairs are . Owning two of those is close to owning one twice.

  4. Rank each stock against the rest

    Average each stock's correlation with the other . A low average marks a stock that often goes its own way.

    What it shows. Hospitals, pharma, oil and FMCG lead (), while move most like the market. This ranking is a starting list, not the answer: see the next step.

  5. Build the basket one stock at a time, one per sector

    Start with the stock that has the lowest average (). Then repeatedly add the stock with the lowest average correlation to the stocks already chosen, taking at most one stock from each sector.

    Why not just take the ten lowest averages. A low average says nothing about how two low-average stocks relate to each other. Building step by step checks every new stock against the ones already in the basket.

    Why one per sector. Companies in the same business share risks that past prices may understate: a drug-pricing rule hits every pharma stock, a banking regulation every bank. The sector cap spreads the basket across different businesses, the closest a stock basket gets to different asset classes.

  6. Choose how many stocks

    Each new stock is the best one left, so the basket's average pair correlation can only climb as it grows: with 2 stocks, with , and with (one from every sector).

    The trade-off. Fewer stocks keep correlation lower, but each company's own news (a bad quarter, a fraud, a regulator's order) becomes a bigger share of the money. Ten stocks is a common middle ground: each holds a tenth of the money, and the basket still sits far below the market's .

  7. Check that it beats chance

    Draw random -stock baskets from the same stocks and measure each the same way.

    Low correlation is not low volatility. An equal-weight basket of these stocks swung a year, against for the median random basket: lower than of them, a smaller gain than the correlation suggests. The chosen stocks swing more on their own ( a year against for the median NIFTY 50 stock), which offsets part of the benefit. Correlation decides how much the stocks offset each other; it does not change how much each one moves.

  8. Test it on years it never saw

    The method was run again on the first three years only (), and the basket it chose was measured on the two years after ().

    Why. A basket picked with five years of hindsight is bound to look good on those five years. The honest test is whether a basket picked in the past stayed less correlated in the future. Correlations drift, so re-check the basket once a year.

What this does not do

  • It does not find an uncorrelated asset class. Every stock here is an Indian large-cap equity, and in a market-wide shock they fall together: on 4 Jun 2024, the election-result day, ONGC fell 16.8 percent, POWERGRID 12.4 percent and ADANIENT 19.3 percent, all three in the basket. A holding that truly moves on its own sits outside equities: the gold ETF GOLDBEES had an average correlation of with these stocks (from to ), lower than any stock basket here.
  • It does not say the basket makes money. Correlation measures whether stocks move together, not whether they rise. The next step is a backtest of the basket (equal weight, rebalanced every year) against NIFTYBEES and the 60:40 NIFTYBEES and GOLDBEES mix.
  • It does not set weights. Every stock counts equally here. Giving steadier stocks more money is a separate step.

Method and data

Data. Daily closes from Historify on the OpenAlgo server, , for of the 50 NIFTY 50 stocks (the 50th constituent was not found in Historify).

Measure. Pearson correlation of daily close-to-close returns; each pair uses the days both stocks traded.

Sectors. Broad groups set by hand (banks, finance and insurance, IT, oil gas and coal, power, FMCG, auto, metals, pharma, hospitals, cement and materials, capital goods and defence, telecom, consumer and retail, transport, diversified). NSE's own industry names differ in places.

Checks. Out of sample: picked on , measured on . Random baskets: draws with a fixed seed, so the figures repeat on every run.
